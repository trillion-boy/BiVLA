\section{Related Work}

We cover three strands: the cost of VLA inference, the training-free
interventions we re-measure, and how such interventions are evaluated.

\textbf{Inference cost in VLA policies.}
VLA policies adapt pretrained vision-language models to output robot actions,
inheriting their size and latency; the resulting efficiency literature already
has surveys of its own~\cite{effvlasurvey2}. We divide it by the resource each
method spends, recovering a recent survey's categories of dynamic computation,
perception and action generation~\cite{effvlasurvey1}:
1)~\emph{when the policy runs} --- executing a predicted chunk over several
control steps, a lineage running from frame skip in RL to parallel
decoding~\cite{dqn, act, diffusionpolicy, openvlaoft};
2)~\emph{what it is shown} --- reducing or reweighting visual tokens;
3)~\emph{how much network each call uses} --- skipping decoder layers. We
treat these as axes rather than competing methods: a claim about efficiency is
a claim about one of these resources being spent differently.

\textbf{The interventions we re-measure.}
Layer redundancy in language models is well established, but its two recipes
disagree on which layers may go. ShortGPT ranks every layer by Block Influence
and constrains nothing~\cite{shortgpt} --- the criterion we adopt --- while
Gromov et al.\ remove a contiguous block of the deepest layers and find the
final layer must be kept~\cite{gromov}. EfficientVLA carries the unconstrained
form into VLAs without training~\cite{efficientvla}; MoLe-VLA needs a learned
router and distillation~\cite{molevla}, placing it outside what we test. Which
rule holds for a policy is open, and our depth results turn on it.
On the visual axis, methods developed for VLMs~\cite{fastv, sparsevlm, tome}
have been reported to transfer poorly to
VLAs: VLA-Cache attributes this to their short action sequences and measures
FastV leaving FLOPs unchanged while latency rises~\cite{vlacache}, and
VLA-Pruner reproduces the degradation on a third setting~\cite{vlapruner}. Our
own visual intervention, foveation, comes from
the other direction: Schwartz's log-polar mapping~\cite{schwartz}, taken up in
robot vision to cut data while preserving central resolution~\cite{traver} and
applied to VLAs by gaze-conditioned policies~\cite{gazereg, lookfocusact}. We
include it because it edits the
observation \emph{before} the encoder, which is what makes it comparable across
backbones: however the encoders differ, each splits the image into a
\emph{uniform} grid, so empty background gets the same budget as the region
where the gripper meets the object. Notably, the newest compact VLAs
build such reductions in by design rather than applying them at
inference~\cite{flower, smolvla, turbovla}, which makes whether a reduction
transfers a question about architecture and not only about a switch.

\textbf{How these claims are evaluated.}
Results are reported on SimplerEnv~\cite{simplerenv} and
LIBERO~\cite{libero}, and recent work addresses the infrastructure: vla-eval
unifies fourteen benchmarks and documents previously undocumented evaluation
pitfalls~\cite{vlaeval}, and StarVLA describes the field as fragmented across
incompatible codebases and protocols~\cite{starvla}. What infrastructure cannot
supply is the comparison itself. Papers using several backbones change the
benchmark at the same time~\cite{vlacache, specprune, vlaiap}, and those with
both axes present leave the crossing cell empty~\cite{gazereg, vlapruner}; the
tables we cite report mean success rates over independent runs, without
matched-episode outcomes. So whether an intervention keeps its \emph{direction}
when the backbone or the benchmark changes cannot be read off these tables, nor
can the per-task disagreements they average over (Table~\ref{tab:reporting}).

We do not cover quantisation, KV-cache compression or learned early exit, which
spend resources our axes do not. Measuring the settings papers leave to their
source code is an established form~\cite{bagoftricks_cnn, bagoftricks_llm}.
Unique to this paper, we measure the same interventions over a complete
backbone $\times$ benchmark grid and test each on matched episodes rather than
aggregate rates, so that the direction of an effect --- not only its size ---
is something the evidence can decide.
