\section{Introduction}

Running a vision-language-action (VLA) policy is expensive. In our own runs a
single forward pass took UniVLA~\cite{univla} $2.80$~s and
SpatialVLA~\cite{spatialvla} $0.90$~s.\footnote{Model time only, averaged over
96 baseline episodes on WidowX-Bridge, on Colab T4/L4-class hardware. A scale
rather than a benchmark: our files do not record which card each run used
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}). Published figures agree on the scale, with
OpenVLA-OFT reporting autoregressive OpenVLA at $3$--$5$~Hz against the
$25$--$50$~Hz its control tasks want~\cite{openvlaoft}.} A literature has grown
up around fixing this without retraining: leave the checkpoint alone and make
inference cheaper by skipping decoder layers, pruning or caching visual tokens,
or holding one predicted action across several control
steps~\cite{efficientvla, vlacache, fastv, shortgpt}. Layer skipping has also
been pursued \emph{with} training~\cite{molevla}, which is outside what we
test.

Such papers converge on one form of claim: \emph{applying method $M$ to
backbone $A$ on benchmark $B$ cut FLOPs by $X\%$ while success fell only $Y$
points, so $M$ is efficient.} That last step holds only if the effect belongs
to the method rather than to the single configuration it was measured in. The
premise is what we test, not any individual method.

We re-measured three interventions under one protocol, on three open backbones
(OpenVLA~\cite{openvla}, SpatialVLA~\cite{spatialvla}, UniVLA~\cite{univla})
and two SimplerEnv~\cite{simplerenv} suites (WidowX-Bridge, Google
Robot/Fractal). We chose them because each spends a different resource:
\emph{action repeat} changes how often the policy runs, \emph{foveation}
changes what it is shown, and \emph{depth pruning} changes how much of the
network each call uses. Five of the six cells are filled, since UniVLA releases
a Bridge-only checkpoint, and each carries eight conditions, for $7{,}198$
episodes in all (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}). Every condition replays the initial
states of its cell's baseline; we count only the episodes whose outcome
flipped, and test those with McNemar's exact test. Because the simulator is
deterministic and the initial states are shared, the resulting $p$-values cover
the sampling of episodes rather than that plus run-to-run drift.

Three results follow, and each has the same shape: what a reported number was
credited to is not what moved it.

\textbf{A choice the specifications leave open moves a result by 45.9 points.}
Inside one cell we held the backbone, the benchmark, the method, the number of
layers removed (four) and the compute saved ($-11\%$) fixed, and varied only
which layers were \emph{eligible} for removal. Success went from $+15.6$ to
$-30.4$ against the same baseline. The ranking criterion never changed. The
candidate window did, and the literature does not agree on how to set it: the
methods we re-measure rank every layer and constrain
nothing~\cite{shortgpt, efficientvla}, while a competing prescription removes a
contiguous block and reports that the final layer must be kept~\cite{gromov}.
The worse of our two settings is the one that deletes it. Run in all five cells
the contrast spans $2.1$ to $50.4$ points, so even the sensitivity is a
property of the configuration.

\textbf{A gain attributed to compression does not come from compression.}
Sweeping how much of the observation foveation keeps, the best setting discards
nothing: $+30.2$ points at $100\%$, against $+18.8$ at $20\%$ and $+4.2$ at
$10\%$. Measured compute moved between
$-3.1\%$ and $+2.7\%$, because neither the image size nor the token count
changes. The gain is reproducible and tracks the log-polar round trip, but it
is an input transformation and not an efficiency technique.

\textbf{In our grid the backbone matters more than the benchmark.} One
benchmark-axis comparison survives correction; six backbone-axis comparisons
do, five of them at $p < 10^{-4}$. On the same $135$ Fractal episodes under the
same intervention, OpenVLA gains $+15.6$ while SpatialVLA loses $-17.8$, each
significant on its own ($p = 4.3 \times 10^{-7}$). The same split is already
sitting in published tables: \texttt{pick coke can} holds up better than
\texttt{move near} in fourteen of the fifteen configurations printed in two
comparison tables~\cite{efficientvla, vlapruner}, spanning four method
families.\footnote{Twelve rows from~\cite{efficientvla} and three
from~\cite{vlapruner}. We exclude the first table's two random-dropping rows,
which are a control rather than a proposed method and which break the pattern.
Three of the four families are independent sources here; the FastV rows are
comparisons run by others, not results FastV reports itself.} The exception is
the one method built to preserve action-relevant tokens~\cite{vlapruner}, which
is what its own mechanism predicts. Those tables do print the per-task numbers.
What none of them does is discuss the split.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\textbf{1)~A uniform re-measurement} of three training-free VLA interventions
over a $3 \times 2$ backbone-benchmark grid: five filled cells, eight
conditions each, $7{,}198$ episodes, with per-episode records released.
\textbf{2)~Evidence that reported effects are often properties of the
configuration}: an eligibility choice the specifications leave open moves one
result by $45.9$ points, and one intervention reverses sign across backbones
with both directions individually significant.
\textbf{3)~Evidence that compute saved does not predict success change}: the
intervention with the largest effect in our grid saves almost none of it, and
helps most when it discards nothing.
\textbf{4)~A measurement procedure} for this class of claim, comprising
episode-level pairing, an explicit determinism check and a grid uniformity
rule, together with Table~\ref{tab:reporting}: every method listed there sweeps
its own configuration, and none publishes episode-level outcomes or tests a
difference for significance.

We propose no new efficiency method, and we do not claim that these
interventions fail in general. Our baselines match published numbers in four of
the five cells and fall $4.2$ points short in the fifth, a gap we could not
trace to a setup error. The claim is narrower: as results are reported now, the
evidence does not separate a property of the method from a property of the
configuration it was measured in.
