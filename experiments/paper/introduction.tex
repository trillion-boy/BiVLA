\section{Introduction}

Running a vision-language-action (VLA) policy is expensive. In our own runs a
single forward pass took UniVLA~\cite{univla} $2.80$~s and
SpatialVLA~\cite{spatialvla} $0.90$~s.\footnote{Model time only, averaged over
96 baseline episodes on WidowX-Bridge, on Colab T4/L4-class hardware. This is a
scale rather than a benchmark, since our files do not record which card each
run used (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}). Published figures agree on the scale.
OpenVLA-OFT reports autoregressive OpenVLA at $3$ to $5$~Hz against the $25$ to
$50$~Hz its control tasks want~\cite{openvlaoft}.} A literature has grown
up around fixing this without retraining. The idea is to leave the checkpoint
alone and make inference cheaper by skipping decoder layers, by pruning or
caching visual tokens, or by holding one predicted action across several
control steps~\cite{efficientvla, vlacache, fastv, shortgpt}. Layer skipping
has also been pursued \emph{with} training~\cite{molevla}, which is outside
what we test.

Such papers converge on one form of claim. \emph{Applying method $M$ to
backbone $A$ on benchmark $B$ cut FLOPs by $X\%$ while success fell only $Y$
points, so $M$ is efficient.} That last step holds only if the effect belongs
to the method rather than to the single configuration it was measured in. The
premise is what we test, not any individual method.

We re-measured three interventions under one protocol, on three open backbones
(OpenVLA~\cite{openvla}, SpatialVLA~\cite{spatialvla}, UniVLA~\cite{univla})
and two SimplerEnv~\cite{simplerenv} suites (WidowX-Bridge, Google
Robot/Fractal). We chose them because each spends a different resource. Action
repeat changes how often the policy runs, foveation (a log-polar warp and its
inverse) changes what it is shown, and depth pruning changes how much of the
network each call uses. Five of the
six cells are filled, since UniVLA releases a Bridge-only checkpoint, and each
carries eight conditions, for $7{,}198$ episodes in all (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}).
Every condition replays the initial states of its cell's baseline. We count
only the episodes whose outcome flipped, and test those with McNemar's exact
test.

Three results follow, and each has the same shape. What a reported number was
credited to is not what moved it.

\textbf{A choice the specifications leave open moves a result by 45.9 points.}
Inside one cell we held the backbone, the benchmark, the method and the number
of layers removed (four) fixed, and varied only which layers were
\emph{eligible} for removal. Success went from $+15.6$ to $-30.4$ against the
same baseline, while the compute saved stayed between $-10.6$ and $-11.9\%$, a
spread no wider than repeating the same measurement produces. The ranking
criterion never changed. The candidate window did, and the literature does not
agree on how to set it. The methods we re-measure rank every layer and
constrain nothing~\cite{shortgpt, efficientvla}, while a competing
prescription removes a contiguous block and reports that the final layer must
be kept~\cite{gromov}.
The worse of our two settings is the one that deletes it. Across all five cells
the contrast spans $2.1$ to $50.4$ points, so even the sensitivity is a
property of the configuration.

\textbf{A gain attributed to compression does not come from compression.}
Sweeping how much of the observation foveation keeps, we find the best setting
discards nothing, giving $+30.2$ points at $100\%$ against $+18.8$ at $20\%$
and $+4.2$
at $10\%$. Measured compute moved between $-3.1\%$ and $+2.7\%$, because
neither the image size nor the token count changes. The gain is reproducible
and tracks the log-polar round trip, but it is an input transformation and not
an efficiency technique.

\textbf{In our grid the backbone matters more than the benchmark.} One
benchmark-axis comparison survives correction. Six backbone-axis comparisons
do, five of them at $p < 10^{-4}$. On the same $135$ Fractal
episodes under the same intervention, OpenVLA gains $+15.6$ while SpatialVLA
loses $-17.8$. Each survives correction on its own, at $p = 0.0011$ and
$p = 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$, and the two differ from each other at
$p = 4.3 \times 10^{-7}$. The same split is already sitting in published
tables. The task \texttt{pick coke can}
holds up better than \texttt{move near} in fourteen of the fifteen
configurations printed in two comparison
tables~\cite{efficientvla, vlapruner}, spanning four method
families.\footnote{Twelve rows from~\cite{efficientvla} and three
from~\cite{vlapruner}. We exclude the first table's two random-dropping rows,
which are a control rather than a proposed method and which break the pattern.
Three of the four families are independent sources here. The FastV rows are
comparisons run by others, not results FastV reports itself.} The exception is
the one method built to preserve action-relevant tokens~\cite{vlapruner}, which
is what its own mechanism predicts. Those tables do print the per-task numbers.
What none of them does is discuss the split.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\textbf{1)~A uniform re-measurement.} Three training-free VLA interventions
over a $3 \times 2$ backbone-benchmark grid, with five filled cells, eight
conditions each, $7{,}198$ episodes, and per-episode records released.
\textbf{2)~Evidence that reported effects are often properties of the
configuration.} An eligibility choice the specifications leave open moves one
result by $45.9$ points, and one intervention reverses sign across backbones
with both directions individually significant.
\textbf{3)~Evidence that compute saved does not predict success change.} The
intervention with the largest effect in our grid saves almost none of it, and
helps most when it discards nothing.
\textbf{4)~A measurement procedure} for this class of claim, comprising
episode-level pairing, an explicit determinism check and a grid uniformity
rule, together with Table~\ref{tab:reporting}. Every method listed there sweeps
its own configuration, and none publishes episode-level outcomes or tests a
difference for significance.

We propose no new efficiency method, and we do not claim that these
interventions fail in general. Four of our five baseline cells sit above the
published figures, one of them by a wide margin, and the fifth is $4.2$ points
below, which is not the pattern a broken setup would produce. Our claim is
narrower. As results are reported
now, the evidence does not separate a property of the method from a property of
the configuration it was measured in.
