\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies are slow. In our own runs a single
forward pass costs UniVLA~\cite{univla} $2.80$~s and
SpatialVLA~\cite{spatialvla} $0.90$~s.\footnote{Model time only, averaged over
96 baseline episodes on WidowX-Bridge; Colab T4/L4-class hardware. A scale, not
a benchmark: our files do not record which card each run used
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).} This has motivated a growing set of
\emph{training-free} interventions that leave the checkpoint untouched and make
inference cheaper --- skipping decoder layers, pruning or caching visual
tokens, and reusing a predicted action across several control
steps~\cite{efficientvla, vlacache, fastv, shortgpt}. Layer skipping has also
been pursued \emph{with} training~\cite{molevla}, which is outside the scope
we test here.

Papers of this kind report a recurring claim: \emph{applying method $M$ to
backbone $A$ on benchmark $B$ cut FLOPs by $X\%$ while success dropped only $Y$
points, therefore $M$ is efficient.} For the last clause to follow, the effect
has to be a property of \emph{the method} rather than of the one configuration
it was measured in. That premise, not any individual method, is what we test.

We re-measure three interventions under one protocol across three open
backbones (OpenVLA~\cite{openvla}, SpatialVLA~\cite{spatialvla},
UniVLA~\cite{univla}) and two SimplerEnv~\cite{simplerenv} suites
(WidowX-Bridge, Google Robot/Fractal). They were chosen to spend different
resources: \emph{action repeat} changes how often the policy runs,
\emph{foveation} changes what it is shown, and \emph{depth pruning} changes how
much of the network each call uses. The grid has five filled cells --- UniVLA
releases a Bridge-only checkpoint --- and eight conditions each, for $7{,}198$
episodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}). Every condition replays the same initial states
as its cell's baseline, and we count only the episodes whose outcome flipped,
testing them with McNemar's exact test; the simulator is deterministic, so the
reported $p$-values are the whole of the uncertainty rather than part of it.

Three results follow, and they have the same shape.

\textbf{An unreported implementation choice moves a result by 45.9 points.}
Holding backbone, benchmark, method, layers removed (four) and compute saved
($-11\%$) fixed inside one cell, and changing only which layers were
\emph{eligible} for removal, success moves from $+15.6$ to $-30.4$ against the
same baseline. The ranking criterion is identical throughout; what differs is
the candidate window, a value the papers in Table~\ref{tab:reporting} do not
report. Repeated in all five cells the same contrast spans $2.1$ to $50.4$
points, so the sensitivity is itself a property of the configuration.

\textbf{A gain attributed to compression does not come from compression.}
Sweeping how much of the observation foveation keeps, the best setting is the
one that discards nothing: $+30.2$ points at $100\%$ against $+18.8$ at $20\%$
and $+4.2$ at $10\%$. Measured compute moves by $-3.1\%$ to $+2.7\%$, because
neither the image size nor the token count changes. The effect is real and
traceable to the log-polar round trip, but it is an input transformation, not
an efficiency technique.

\textbf{Changing the backbone moves results more than changing the benchmark.}
One benchmark-axis comparison survives correction; six backbone-axis
comparisons do, five at $p < 10^{-4}$. On the same $135$ Fractal episodes with
the same intervention, OpenVLA gains $+15.6$ and SpatialVLA loses $-17.8$, each
significant alone ($p = 4.3 \times 10^{-7}$). The same split is already visible
in published tables: \texttt{pick coke can} rises while \texttt{move near}
falls in all twelve configurations reported across two author groups and three
method families~\cite{efficientvla, vlacache, fastv}, and none of those papers
discusses it, because all report only the four-task average.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\textbf{1)~A uniform re-measurement} of three training-free VLA interventions
over a $3 \times 2$ backbone-benchmark grid --- five filled cells, eight
conditions each, $7{,}198$ episodes --- with per-episode records released.
\textbf{2)~Evidence that reported effects are often properties of the
configuration}: an unreported eligibility flag moves one result by $45.9$
points, and one intervention reverses sign across backbones with both
directions individually significant.
\textbf{3)~Evidence that compute saved does not predict success change}: the
intervention with the largest effect in our grid saves approximately none, and
helps most when it discards nothing.
\textbf{4)~A measurement procedure} for this class of claim --- episode-level
pairing, an explicit determinism check, and a grid uniformity rule --- together
with the reporting gaps in Table~\ref{tab:reporting} that make such claims
unreproducible.

We propose no new efficiency method and do not claim these interventions fail
in general; our baselines match published numbers in four of five cells and
fall $4.2$ points short in the fifth, which does not fit a systematic setup
error. The claim is narrower: the evidence currently offered does not
distinguish a property of the method from a property of the configuration it
was measured in.
